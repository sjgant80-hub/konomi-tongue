Konomi · the tongue the agents grow

The Konomi Tongue

Can AI agents that work together talk in fewer tokens by sharing a dictionary? Tokens are what a language model reads and bills, so fewer is cheaper and faster. This is that question, measured in the open with the rules sealed first — and the answer: new conversation barely shrinks (1.05×), JSON records shrink (2.72× with the schema held), dense symbol pictures never read back at the bar, and the 15× target was reached only by repeated work — a LIGHT template called by name (best 50.1×) — never by new talk. The part that paid — a JSON pack that carries its own legend — is shipped where it measured cheaper.

What it isA shared dictionary, measured in tokens

Agents in the estate send each other, and a language model, a great deal of text and JSON. The tongue grows a dictionary from what they actually say: the most valuable recurring pair of pieces becomes a single glyph (a kanji or kana that costs one or two tokens), again and again, so a glyph can stand for a phrase made of glyphs. JSON gets a shared schema: a record shape, a key or a string that recurs becomes one glyph. And a picture channel tests whether symbols drawn as an image — billed by area, not by token — carry more per token. Every channel was measured on held-out material, in real Claude tokens, against Simon's 15× target.

3 of 11 sealed rules held.

Grown to 4723 glyphs, the tongue carried the held-out agent messages in 1.05× fewer Claude tokens and the held-out JSON in 2.72×, against Simon's 15×. Drawn as a picture, the best arm carried 0 bits per token read back at ≥99%, against 6.98 for the best text; the coded messages drawn as pictures cost 2.07× fewer tokens than their English.

What limits it
The held-out messages cost 16580 Claude tokens plain; the grown dictionary saved 751 of them (4.5%). Four things hold it back. New information: most of what an agent says has not been said before, and a dictionary only saves what recurs. Cheap glyphs are scarce: of the 4723 glyphs the tongue could write, only 166 were planned at one token, so a two-token glyph pays only for a phrase of three tokens or more — and Claude's tokenizer already spends about one token on a common English word. Holding the dictionary costs 44319 tokens, more than the held-out messages themselves. And Claude decoded 2 of 8 coded messages exactly. In pictures, dense grids of random symbols — raw dots or kanji — never read back at 99% at any size tried; plain text drawn at 12 px did, and that is where the picture gain comes from.

What was sealed first


Sealed, committed and pushed before any held-out message is coded or any picture is read. The result is published whichever way it lands; where it falls short of 15×, this says how far it got and what limits it.


Text. units (words with one leading space, digit runs, whitespace runs, single characters) merged pair by pair: each round the 16 most valuable pairs that share no symbol (count × tokens saved, priced) become new glyphs, for 512 rounds or until the alphabet runs out; a glyph may hold glyphs (nesting). The curve is read at 0, 64, 256, 1024, 4096 glyphs and at the full dictionary.
JSON. a recurring ordered key list (schema), key or string value becomes one glyph; records write as S(v1,v2,…); every held-out file must decode to its exact minified JSON
Picture. arm A raw dots (48×48 black/white squares in nested blocks of 3, dot sizes 8/6/4/3/2 px); arm B familiar glyphs (24×16 grid of 256 one-token kanji spread across the priced range, cell sizes 28/20/14/10/8 px, Yu Gothic); arm C the same grid with the alphabet evolved two generations against the model's own training misreads (every misread glyph replaced by a fresh one-token kanji, seeded). 2 held-out images per arm and size, payloads seeded and never seen before the read. Bits per token = payload bits ÷ image tokens (the read's input tokens minus the same prompt without the image), at the smallest size where every held-out image reads back at ≥99%. Nesting depth curve: raw dots at 6 px, depth 1–4 (3^d × 3^d dots).
Both. 6 held-out messages (seeded) coded with the full dictionary and drawn as text at 16/12/9 px; each read back blind; the ratio is their English tokens ÷ the image tokens at each one's smallest size read back at ≥99% (whitespace runs count as one space)


The split. by time: every coordinator↔Kar message before 2026-10-01T12:00:00.000Z and every memory file last written before it trains the dictionary; every message at or after it is held out. The corpus stays on the laptop — only its sha256 and counts are published.


Before the seal: the glyph prices (their own measurement, committed), a plumbing check of the CLI image path on two images of known content (an 8-kanji grid read exactly; an 18-square dot grid misread — raw dots at 6 px with outlines), and the corpus counts. No held-out message was coded and no payload picture was read. The alphabet was decided by price. In Claude’s tokenizer: cuneiform 922 priced: 922 at 4 tokens; LIGHT’s opcodes, rings and buses 48 priced: 8 at 2 tokens, 38 at 3 tokens, 2 at 4 tokens; the seed’s glyphs 163 priced: 65 at 1 token, 64 at 2 tokens, 26 at 3 tokens, 6 at 4 tokens, 2 at 5 tokens; kanji U+4E00–U+51FF 1024 priced: 153 at 1 token, 871 at 2 tokens; kana 176 priced: 28 at 1 token, 56 at 2 tokens, 42 at 3 tokens, 50 at 4 tokens; kanji U+5200–U+61FF (batches only, admitted at ≤2 on average, planned as 2) 3648 priced: 3648 at 2 tokens. So the text channel writes kanji and kana; cuneiform and emoji only ever pay off in the picture channel, where a glyph costs area, not tokens. Pricing was stopped at these ranges for time (one CLI call per glyph is about 2.6 s). LIGHT (Thomas Frumkin, teslasolar/light, used with permission) has 36 opcodes in src/ops.js at the forked commit (its README says 38); its prime mapping is carried in the legend. The tongue's dictionary is modelled on LIGHT's templates (a whole program called by name); LIGHT's runtime itself is not on the measured path. A mock run of the whole pipeline (no paid calls: counts as characters ÷ 4, every reply empty, the 22 held-out messages replaced by the last 22 training messages) checked the plumbing end to end before the seal; it did touch the held-out JSON files, whose character ratio (3.45×, not tokens) was therefore seen. No prediction was changed after it. The 15× is Simon's target, sealed as the bar. Claude tokens are counted for claude-sonnet-5-5; the picture reads use the same model.

For a teamWhat the tongue does for a company that sends records to a model


If your systems send structured records — tool results, queues, ledgers, run records — to a language model, the shipped JSON pack can cut the input tokens they cost, with no shared state to keep in step: the legend travels with the record, and the reader gets back exactly what was sent. Measured in the estate: 12 of 13 places cheaper packed. It is MIT, zero-dependency and runs on your own machines.


What it does for each role



 
CEO
A small, owned saving on the model bill, from a method whose every claim was written down before it was measured — including the 8 that failed. What the board hears is what was measured.

 
CFO
The money is input tokens, computed below from your own volume and your own price. Straight: the saving scales with volume, and at modest volumes it is small. Minifying JSON is free and takes part of the same gain first.

 
COO
Switch it on one place at a time, only where it measures cheaper, and keep the plain form as the fallback; the reader unpacks to the exact record, so nothing downstream changes.

 
CTO / CISO
A pure, zero-dependency ES module, mutation-gated on GitHub's runner on every push. It does no I/O and calls no network; it changes how a record is written, not who receives it.

 
Engineering teams
pack(value, alphabet), unpack(text) and wins(today, packed) — three functions. Lossless by construction and by test; garbage in gives { ok: false }, never a throw.

 
Data and compliance teams
A packed record holds the same data as the plain one, in a reversible form: personal data stays personal data, and it reaches the same model provider it reached before.

Against what you use now


The standard alternative is free: send minified JSON instead of pretty-printed. On the same 13 places, minifying alone is where part of the gain comes from; packing goes further on 10 of the 13 even against minified JSON (median place 81% of the minified tokens), and on 12 against what is sent today (median 67.1%). The model must read the legend: in the read check Claude answered 2 of 2 questions exactly from the packed form, as it did from the plain — a small check, so measure it on your own questions. The text dictionary does not pay against plain English (1.05×), and on new talk nothing here reaches 15×.



Security and data protection


    
 
  • Where the data is. The kernels run on your machines and in this tab; they do no I/O, read no clock and call no network. Packing changes how a record is written, not where it goes.
    • 
 
  • Exactly what was sent. unpack(pack(v)) is v — by construction and by test, and on every measured place (13 of 13). A pack uses only glyphs that never occur in the value, so a glyph can only be a glyph. Garbage is refused with a reason, never a throw.
    • 
 
  • It does not sanitise. Whatever a record carries — including text written to steer a model — the model reads after unpacking, exactly as it would read the plain record. Treat packed input with the same controls as plain input.
    • 
 
  • Not encryption, not anonymisation. The legend travels with the record, so anyone who reads the pack can read the data. Personal data in a pack is still personal data under UK GDPR, your controller duties are unchanged, and the model provider you send it to receives the same data as before.
    • 
 
  • Assurance you can re-run. The source is MIT and open; the tests, the seals, the record re-derivation and the mutation gate run on GitHub's runner on every push, and on your machine with the commands in Start here.
    •

The case for the board


The tongue is a measured answer, not a promise: a shared dictionary does not make new conversation meaningfully cheaper, and the record says so. What did pay is small, owned and safe to try — a JSON pack that carries its own legend, cuts the input tokens of repetitive records (the median measured place keeps 67.1%), returns exactly what was sent, and is switched on only where it measures cheaper. It costs nothing to licence and runs without a supplier. Adopt it where your systems send a model the same shapes again and again, measure it on your own records first, and treat the method — rules sealed before the measurement, failures kept — as the larger asset.







AdoptionHow a team rolls it out


The pack fits a team whose systems send a language model structured records with recurring shapes: tool results, queues, ledgers, run records. It is not for free text, and it does not replace minifying — do that first.


    
 
  1. Try it on your own records, here. Paste one into Try it. Nothing leaves the tab.
    2. 
 
  2. Run the gates on your own machine. Clone the repository and run the commands in Start here, and the mutation gate as gate.yml runs it. You are trusting what you just re-ran, not this page.
    3. 
 
  3. List the places. Each tool result, file or message your systems send a model is one place, as tools/ship.mjs lists the estate's.
    4. 
 
  4. Write down what you expect before you measure. Commit the places and the rule for switching on — as data/ship-prereg.json was sealed — so the result cannot be tuned after the fact.
    5. 
 
  5. Measure each place three ways in your model's tokens: as sent today, minified, and packed with the legend included. Keep every result, including the places where it loses.
    6. 
 
  6. Check the model reads it. Ask a question with a fixed answer from the plain form and from the pack, as the ship run's read check did.
    7. 
 
  7. Switch on only where it wins. Use wins(today, packed); leave every other place as it is, and keep the plain form as the fallback.
    8. 
 
  8. Gate it in your CI. Copy .github/workflows/gate.yml: the tests, the seal check, the record re-derivation and the pinned mutation gate.
    9.

White paperA language grown between agents, measured against its own target




Abstract


We grew a nested glyph dictionary from 361 real messages between two of the estate's agents, a shared JSON schema from the estate's own data, and a picture channel read back blind, and measured each in Claude tokens on held-out material against a 15× target, with 11 rules and a prediction sealed in git before any held-out message was coded. 3 of 11 rules held. The dictionary carried 22 held-out messages in 1.05× fewer tokens; the schema carried 6 held-out JSON files in 2.72×; the best picture arm carried 0 bits per token at the read-back bar, against 6.98 for the best text. Every held-out message and file decoded exactly. A per-value JSON pack, measured where it lands, was cheaper on 12 of 13 places and is shipped there.


1 · The question


Agents that work together repeat themselves: the same headings, the same record shapes, the same names. A language model bills by the token, so a vocabulary both sides already hold should make a message cheaper, the way a template — a whole program called by name — makes a call cheap. The question is how much cheaper on what they actually say next, which they have not said before.


2 · Method


Every candidate glyph was priced in Claude tokens first, so the alphabet is chosen by cost. The material was split by time: everything before 2026-10-01 trains, everything after is held out. The rules, the prices, the split, the prompts and a prediction were sealed (59d37fe) before any held-out message was coded or any picture read. The dictionary grows by merging the most valuable adjacent pair of units, where value is how many tokens the merge saves at the glyph's real price; JSON keys, strings and record shapes become glyphs by length times count. Pictures are seeded payloads drawn at shrinking sizes and read back blind; a size counts only if every held-out image reads back at ≥99%. CI re-grades every recorded read and re-derives every number on every push.


3 · Results


Text: 1.05× (16,580 → 15,829 tokens), climbing slowly with the dictionary and not at every step. Claude decoded 2 of 8 coded messages exactly from the legend. JSON: 2.72× (64,519 → 23,745), all 6 files exact. Pictures: no arm of dots or glyphs read back at the bar at any size tried, so the best arm carried 0 bits per token. The coded messages drawn as plain text pictures cost 2.07× fewer tokens than their English, 6 of 6 read back at ≥99%. Shipped: the pack was cheaper than today's text on 12 of 13 places (median place 67.1% of its tokens), lossless on all, and read as well as the plain form in the read check.


4 · Why it falls short of 15×


New information: most of what an agent says next has not been said before, and a dictionary saves only what recurs. Cheap glyphs are scarce, so a two-token glyph pays only for a phrase of three tokens or more, while Claude's tokenizer already spends about one token on a common English word. Holding the dictionary costs more tokens than the held-out messages themselves. And in pictures, dense grids of unfamiliar symbols never read back at 99% at the sizes tried.


5 · What shipped


The JSON pack: a value learns its own legend from itself, so no shared state is needed; it is switched on only where it measured cheaper than what is sent today. LIGHT's templates, called by name, show where large ratios do live — on repeated work, a program both sides already hold: best 50.1× (translate), median 3.75× over 15.


6 · Limits


One pair of agents, one tokenizer, one model reading pictures. The held-out set is small (22 messages, 6 JSON files). The pack's read check is two questions. The 15× chase stays a standing, measured line (tools/point.mjs), never a claim.









QuestionsAsked and answered


What is the Konomi Tongue?
A language the estate's agents grow between themselves: a shared dictionary where the most valuable recurring pieces of what they actually say become glyphs, glyphs can hold glyphs, and JSON records shed their keys into a shared schema — plus a picture channel that draws symbols as images, which are billed by area rather than by token.
Did it reach 15×?
3 of 11 sealed rules held. Grown to 4723 glyphs, the tongue carried the held-out agent messages in 1.05× fewer Claude tokens and the held-out JSON in 2.72×, against Simon's 15×. Drawn as a picture, the best arm carried 0 bits per token read back at ≥99%, against 6.98 for the best text; the coded messages drawn as pictures cost 2.07× fewer tokens than their English.
Why kanji and not cuneiform?
Price. Every candidate glyph was counted through the CLI first: in Claude's tokenizer a cuneiform sign costs 4 tokens and most emoji 3–4, while many kanji cost 1 or 2. Cuneiform and emoji can still pay in the picture channel, where a glyph costs area.
How do I know it was not tuned after the fact?
The prices, the corpus split, the channels, the rules and a prediction were committed before any held-out message was coded or any picture read (data/prereg.json). CI re-grades every recorded read and rebuilds the JSON channel on every push; this page re-grades the reads in your browser.
Whose ideas are these?
The tongue grows from the seed's memory law (compress, don't delete; compress by prime indices) and Thomas Frumkin's Konomi architecture and LIGHT language (36 prime-indexed opcodes; templates — a whole program called by name — are the model for the dictionary). Simon set the 15× target and the picture channel.
Can my team use it?
Yes, the part that paid: the JSON pack (tongue-pack.mjs), MIT and zero-dependency. It is worth switching on where your systems send a model records with recurring shapes, and only where it measures cheaper on your own records — the cost case and the adoption steps on this page say how. The text dictionary and the picture channel did not pay and are not offered for use. Supplied by AI Native Solutions; support is agreed per deployment.
